## Chapter 4. Collaborative Evaluation Practices

Error analysis ([Chapter 3](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/evals-for-ai/9798341660717/ch03.html#sec_error_analysis)) gives us a list of failure modes. The next question is whether different people on the team agree on what qualifies as a failure. This chapter covers how to align human judgment.

In the previous chapter ([Chapter 3](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/evals-for-ai/9798341660717/ch03.html#sec_error_analysis)), we walked through error analysis: reading traces, identifying failure modes, and building a taxonomy of how your system goes wrong. That process depends on human judgment at every step. You decide what counts as a failure. You decide how to categorize errors. You decide whether a trace is acceptable or not.

Error analysis ([Chapter 3](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/evals-for-ai/9798341660717/ch03.html#sec_error_analysis)) depends on human judgment at every step. You decide what qualifies as a failure, and how to categorize errors. But evaluation criteria are often subjective (what one person considers “helpful,” another might find verbose), and individual judgment is inconsistent (the same person might rate the same trace differently on different days). If the labels are unreliable, everything built on top of them (failure mode counts, automated evaluators, improvement decisions) inherits that noise.

This chapter covers *collaborative evaluation practices*: structured approaches for getting multiple people to agree on what qualifies as a pass or a fail.

As a motivating example, Notion runs evaluations across roughly 70 engineers and built a dedicated team whose job is to define what “good” looks like for each AI feature and write it down as a rubric.[^1] Their framework accounts for *criteria drift*, the fact that what qualifies as “good” shifts over time as the team’s understanding of users and edge cases deepens. Shankar et al. (\[Link to Come\], Shankar et al. 2024) studied the same phenomenon in a controlled setting and found that developers routinely revised past labels as they saw more examples. Evaluation criteria are not fixed. They evolve as the team sees more data, and the rubrics have to evolve with them.

## When a Single Expert Is Enough

Before diving into collaborative workflows, it is worth asking: *do you actually need multiple annotators?*

In many cases, the answer is no. In small to medium-sized companies, it is often more effective to appoint a single principal domain expert—someone whose judgment serves as the definitive standard for evaluation.[^2]

We call this the “benevolent dictator” model, borrowing a term from open-source software governance. Just as some successful open-source projects have a single maintainer whose judgment is final, some evaluation efforts benefit from having one person whose word is the “ground truth”.

The benevolent dictator model works well when a few conditions hold:

- There is a clear authority figure whose judgment the team trusts. This person defines not only what is acceptable technically but also helps you understand whether you are building something users actually want. When one person’s judgment is the standard, there are no annotation conflicts to resolve.
- The evaluation criteria are within that person’s expertise. By involving them throughout the evaluation process, you uncover preferences and expectations that they might not be able to articulate upfront. The process of reviewing traces helps them clarify what a “passable” AI interaction looks like.
- The volume of labeling work is manageable for one person. This keeps the process fast and simple—and gives the expert a stake in the AI’s development. They feel invested because they have had a hand in shaping it, which makes them more likely to champion the product.

Who might serve as a benevolent dictator? Some examples:

- A psychologist for a mental health AI assistant
- A lawyer for an AI that analyzes legal documents
- A customer service director for a support chatbot
- A lead teacher or curriculum developer for an educational AI tool
- The CEO or founder in a small startup

###### Note

If you are an independent developer, you are the domain expert by default. But be honest with yourself about your expertise—and regularly validate your assumptions against real user feedback.

If you must rely on leadership as your domain expert, make sure their judgments reflect actual user needs, not just their personal preferences.

Many developers attempt to act as the domain expert themselves, or find a convenient proxy (such as their manager). This can be a recipe for disaster. People throughout an organization will have varying opinions about what is acceptable, and it is impossible to make everyone happy. The benevolent dictator model works precisely because it cuts through this ambiguity by designating a single source of truth.

The benevolent dictator model has limits, however. If the domain is too complex for one person to evaluate comprehensively, if the criteria are contested among stakeholders, or if you need to demonstrate that your evaluation process is rigorous and unbiased, you will need to involve multiple annotators. The rest of this chapter covers how to do that effectively.

## A Collaborative Annotation Workflow

When a single expert is not sufficient, you need a structured process for involving multiple people in evaluation. This section outlines a workflow for collaboratively defining and refining evaluation criteria. The goal is to produce clear, consistent rubrics that multiple people—and eventually automated evaluators—can apply reliably.

*Should your team tackle one criterion at a time or several at once?* In practice, most teams annotate multiple criteria in a single pass through the data. If you are already reviewing a trace, it is efficient to label that trace for factual correctness, tone, completeness, and any other criteria that matter, all at once. However, when you measure agreement and run alignment sessions, treat each criterion separately. Two annotators might both recognize when a response contains factual errors, but have completely different intuitions about what qualifies as “too formal” or “too casual” in tone. If you compute a single agreement score across all criteria, you will not know which rubrics are working and which need refinement. Measure agreement per criterion, and run alignment sessions focused on one criterion at a time.

Our collaborative evaluation workflow has nine steps:

Step 1: Assemble the annotation team

Select two or more annotators with relevant domain expertise or stakeholder perspective. Clarify their roles and the scope of the annotation task from the outset. In smaller teams, you might have just two people; in larger organizations, you might involve representatives from engineering, product, legal, and customer support.

Step 2: Draft initial rubrics

Create first versions of your rubrics—one per evaluation criterion. Each rubric should include a working definition (for example, “Is the response factually accurate with respect to the source document?”) and a few illustrative Pass/Fail examples. These drafts often emerge from earlier error analysis—you might be formalizing failure modes you identified while reading traces yourself.

Step 3: Select a shared annotation set

Curate a common set of 20-50 representative traces that all annotators will label. Choose examples that are directly relevant to the criteria you are evaluating, and deliberately include borderline or ambiguous cases. These edge cases are where the rubrics get tested—if everyone agrees on the easy cases but disagrees on the hard ones, you know the rubrics need work.

Step 4: Label independently

Each annotator labels all examples on their own, using the draft rubrics. No discussion is allowed during this phase. The goal is to surface differences in how annotators interpret the rubrics—not to reach consensus yet. If annotators talk during this phase, their judgments become correlated, and you lose the ability to detect ambiguities.

Step 5: Measure inter-annotator agreement

After labeling, compute inter-annotator agreement (IAA) scores for each criterion separately, using metrics like Cohen’s Kappa (discussed in the next section). Low agreement on a particular criterion flags that its rubric is vague or that annotators are interpreting it differently. A criterion with high agreement is ready to move forward; one with low agreement needs more work.

Step 6: Facilitate alignment sessions

Bring annotators together to discuss cases where they disagreed. Focus on one criterion at a time—mixing discussions of factual accuracy and tone in the same conversation creates confusion. The goal is to understand the source of disagreement and improve the rubric, not to argue about past labels. We cover alignment sessions in detail later in this chapter.

Step 7: Revise the rubrics

Update each rubric based on insights from the alignment discussion. Clarify definitions, refine examples, and adjust labeling criteria as needed. The rubrics should become more precise with each iteration.

Step 8: Iterate

Repeat the process: select new examples, label independently, measure IAA per criterion, and revise. Continue until agreement reaches acceptable levels—typically a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.6 or higher for each criterion you care about.

Step 9: Finalize rubrics and labels

Once agreement is consistently high, document the final rubrics and generate a consensus-labeled dataset. This “gold standard” set serves two purposes: it validates that humans can apply the rubrics consistently, and it provides ground truth for evaluating automated LLM-as-Judge evaluators (which we cover in the next chapter).

## Measuring Inter-Annotator Agreement

How do you know whether your annotators are applying the rubric consistently? You measure their agreement.

## Percent Agreement: Simple but Misleading

The simplest measure is *percent agreement*: the proportion of items where both annotators assigned the same label. If two annotators each labeled 100 traces and agreed on 85 of them, the percent agreement is 85%.

Mathematically, percent agreement (also called observed agreement, P <sub>o</sub>) is:

$$
P_{o} = \frac{\text{number of items where both annotators agree}}{\text{total number of items}}
$$

Percent agreement is easy to compute and easy to interpret. But it has a serious flaw: it does not account for agreement that could happen by chance.

Consider a scenario where 95% of your traces are “Pass” and only 5% are “Fail.” If both annotators simply labeled everything as “Pass,” they would agree on 95% of traces—even though neither is actually reading the traces carefully. The high agreement would be an artifact of the skewed label distribution, not evidence that the rubric is clear.

The label skew becomes more severe as your system improves. Early in development, you might have a roughly even mix of Pass and Fail traces, so chance agreement is around 50%. But as you fix bugs and the system gets better, more traces become Pass. If 90% of traces are Pass, chance agreement rises to about 81% (0.9 × 0.9 + 0.1 × 0.1). A percent agreement of 85% would sound good, but it barely exceeds what you would expect from random labeling.

## Cohen’s Kappa: Adjusting for Chance

Cohen’s Kappa (κ) addresses this problem by comparing observed agreement to expected chance agreement Cohen 1960. The formula is:

$$
\kappa = \frac{P_{o} - P_{e}}{1 - P_{e}}
$$

Here, P <sub>o</sub> is the observed percent agreement, and P <sub>e</sub> is the expected agreement if both annotators were labeling randomly (but with their actual label distributions).

To compute P <sub>e</sub>, you calculate how often each annotator uses each label, then compute the probability that they would agree by chance. For binary labels (Pass/Fail):

$$
P_{e} = \left(P_{\text{Pass} , 1} \times P_{\text{Pass} , 2}\right) + \left(P_{\text{Fail} , 1} \times P_{\text{Fail} , 2}\right)
$$

A Kappa of 1 means perfect agreement—the annotators agreed on everything, and none of that agreement is attributable to chance. A Kappa of 0 means the annotators agreed exactly as often as chance would predict—their agreement is meaningless. A negative Kappa means they agreed less often than chance, which suggests systematic disagreement (perhaps they are using opposite interpretations of the rubric).

## Interpreting Kappa Scores

A standard interpretation of Kappa values, following Landis and Koch 1977, is:

- < 0: Poor (worse than chance)
- 0.00-0.20: Slight
- 0.21-0.40: Fair
- 0.41-0.60: Moderate
- 0.61-0.80: Substantial
- 0.81-1.00: Almost perfect

*In practice, aim for κ ≥ 0.6* before treating your labels as reliable. Below that threshold, the rubric likely needs refinement—annotators are interpreting it too differently to produce consistent labels.

If your Kappa is significantly below 0.6, do not continue blindly labeling more traces. You will just be producing inconsistent data. Look at the specific traces where annotators disagreed. In the real estate example above, Annotators B and C both read the same response about a duplex being “popular with families,” but B saw it as a persona mismatch while C saw it as relevant tenant-demand context. That disagreement highlighted that the rubric never specified whether family-oriented language qualifies as investor-appropriate when it describes rental appeal. A quick discussion of this one trace led to an explicit decision rule and improvement to the rubric. Without that calibration, the team would have continued labeling inconsistently.

Some failure modes are also inherently harder to agree on. Factual errors tend to produce high agreement—either the response says the property has 3 bedrooms or it doesn’t. But judgments about tone, persona fit, or “appropriateness” often don’t. In the example above, “client persona match” hit a Kappa of only 0.3 or 0.4 on the first pass. The team improved alignment by splitting “client persona match” into two separate criteria: one for whether the response includes information relevant to the client’s goals (like cap rates for investors), and another for whether it avoids language that sounds like it’s addressing a different audience. Annotators had been agreeing on the first question but disagreeing on the second, so separating them revealed where the rubric actually needed work.

## Python Implementation

Here is a Python implementation of Cohen’s Kappa:

```
from collections import Counter

def cohens_kappa(rater1, rater2):
    """
    Compute Cohen's Kappa for two lists of categorical ratings.

    Args:
        rater1: List of labels from first annotator
        rater2: List of labels from second annotator

    Returns:
        float: Cohen's Kappa score
    """
    assert len(rater1) == len(rater2), "Rating lists must have same length"
    n_items = len(rater1)

    if n_items == 0:
        return 1.0  # Convention: perfect agreement if nothing to rate

    # Observed agreement
    agreements = sum(1 for i in range(n_items) if rater1[i] == rater2[i])
    p_observed = agreements / n_items

    # Expected agreement by chance
    labels = set(rater1) | set(rater2)
    count1 = Counter(rater1)
    count2 = Counter(rater2)

    p_expected = sum(
        (count1.get(label, 0) / n_items) * (count2.get(label, 0) / n_items)
        for label in labels
    )

    # Handle edge case where expected agreement is 1
    if p_expected == 1.0:
        return 1.0 if p_observed == 1.0 else 0.0

    return (p_observed - p_expected) / (1 - p_expected)

# Example usage
annotations_a = ['Pass', 'Fail', 'Pass', 'Pass', 'Fail', 'Pass', 'Fail']
annotations_b = ['Pass', 'Fail', 'Fail', 'Pass', 'Fail', 'Pass', 'Pass']
print(f"Cohen's Kappa: {cohens_kappa(annotations_a, annotations_b):.3f}")
```

## When to Use Other Metrics

Cohen’s Kappa is designed for a specific situation: two annotators rating the same items with categorical labels. When your setup differs, other metrics may be more appropriate.

*More than two annotators*. If three or more people are labeling the same traces, use Fleiss’ Kappa, which generalizes Cohen’s Kappa to multiple raters. Where Cohen’s Kappa compares one pair of annotators, Fleiss’ Kappa measures the average agreement across all items, adjusted for chance. Like Cohen’s Kappa, the formula is:

$$
\kappa = \frac{\left(\bar{P}\right)_{o} - \left(\bar{P}\right)_{e}}{1 - \left(\bar{P}\right)_{e}}
$$

Here, P̄ <sub>o</sub> is the average observed agreement across all items (computed by looking at how often annotators assigned the same label to each item), and P̄ <sub>e</sub> is the expected agreement by chance (computed from the overall distribution of labels across all annotators). The interpretation is the same as Cohen’s Kappa—values above 0.6 indicate substantial agreement—but the calculation accounts for comparing across more than one pair.

*Ordinal ratings.* If your labels have a natural order (like a 1-5 quality scale), standard Kappa treats all disagreements as equally bad: rating something a 4 vs. a 5 counts the same as rating it a 1 vs. a 5. That’s often not what you want—being one point off is less serious than being four points off. Weighted Kappa addresses this by assigning different weights to different levels of disagreement. The formula is:

$$
\kappa_{w} = \frac{P_{o} \left(w\right) - P_{e} \left(w\right)}{1 - P_{e} \left(w\right)}
$$

The weighted observed agreement P <sub>o</sub> (w) and weighted expected agreement P <sub>e</sub> (w) are computed using a weight matrix that specifies how “bad” each type of disagreement is. A common choice is quadratic weights, where the penalty for disagreement grows with the square of the distance between labels: a 1 vs. 5 disagreement is penalized 16 times more than a 1 vs. 2 disagreement.

That said, we generally recommend binary Pass/Fail labels over ordinal scales. With a 5-point scale, you need to define what distinguishes a 3 from a 4, and annotators often have different intuitions about these boundaries. Binary labels force you to make one clear decision: does this trace meet the bar or not? If your application is mature enough that you need finer-grained quality distinctions—say, you’re prioritizing *which* acceptable responses to improve further—ordinal scales can be useful. But start with binary labels until you have a specific reason to need more granularity.

*Missing data.* In practice, not every annotator labels every trace. Someone gets sick, or you deliberately split the work so that only a subset of annotators sees each item. Cohen’s Kappa assumes complete overlap; both annotators must have labeled all the same items. Krippendorff’s Alpha handles missing data gracefully. The formula is:

$$
\alpha = 1 - \frac{D_{o}}{D_{e}}
$$

Here, D <sub>o</sub> is the observed disagreement (computed from the actual labels) and D <sub>e</sub> is the expected disagreement by chance. Unlike Kappa, which computes agreement, Alpha computes disagreement and subtracts from 1. This formulation naturally handles missing data because it only considers pairs of labels that actually exist. Alpha also works with any number of annotators and any measurement scale (binary, ordinal, interval, or ratio), making it a flexible default when your annotation setup is complex.

Alpha values are interpreted similarly to Kappa: 1 means perfect agreement, 0 means agreement no better than chance, and negative values mean systematic disagreement. Krippendorff recommends α ≥ 0.8 for reliable data and suggests treating α between 0.67 and 0.8 as acceptable for tentative conclusions. These thresholds are stricter than the typical Kappa guidance, so don’t be alarmed if your Alpha scores come out a bit lower than you’d expect.

For most binary or categorical labeling tasks with two raters and complete coverage, Cohen’s Kappa is the right choice; it’s simple to compute and widely understood. When your situation is more complex, Krippendorff’s Alpha is a flexible alternative that handles multiple raters, ordinal scales, and missing data in a single framework.

###### Note

We haven’t introduced LLM-as-Judge evaluators yet; that’s the focus of the next chapter. But since we’re discussing agreement metrics, it’s worth clarifying a common mistake: *using Cohen’s Kappa to measure how well an LLM judge matches human-generated “ground truth” labels.* Cohen’s Kappa measures agreement between two peers; neither is assumed to be “correct.” This is appropriate when two humans are developing a rubric together; you want to know whether they interpret the rubric the same way, not which one is right.

Once you have established gold-standard human labels, you have a source of truth. If you want to know whether an LLM judge matches those labels, you’re no longer asking “do these two raters agree?” You’re asking “does this judge get the true answer?” That’s a classification problem, and you need to know which way the judge is wrong. Did the judge say Pass when the human said Fail (a false positive)? Or Fail when the human said Pass (a false negative)? A judge that’s too lenient will miss bad outputs; a judge that’s too strict will flag good outputs for unnecessary review. Classification metrics like precision, recall, and true/false positive rates let you diagnose these failure modes separately. Cohen’s Kappa just tells you humans and LLM judges disagree, so don’t make the mistake of using it for judge *evaluation.*

## Facilitating Alignment Sessions

When annotators disagree, you need to understand why. Alignment sessions are structured discussions where annotators review their disagreements and work together to improve the rubric.

## Preparing for the Session

Before bringing annotators together:

*Calculate and share IAA scores.* Let everyone see the overall agreement level so they understand the scope of the problem. For example, you might report: “On the *factual accuracy* criterion, we achieved a Kappa of 0.74—substantial agreement. On *appropriate tone for client persona,* we achieved a Kappa of 0.38—fair agreement. We will focus the alignment session on tone.”

*Identify the specific disagreements.* Pull out the traces where annotators gave different labels. Prepare a summary document listing each disputed trace, the labels each annotator assigned, and any notes they provided.

To make this concrete, consider the real estate CRM assistant from the previous chapter, which helps agents search listings, schedule showings, and draft client emails. Suppose your team is evaluating whether the assistant’s responses are “appropriate for the client persona.” The initial rubric might look like this:

```
Criterion: Client Persona Match

Pass: The response is tailored to the client's background and goals.
      An investor should receive information relevant to investment
      decisions. A first-time homebuyer should receive information
      that helps them understand the buying process.

Fail: The response ignores or mismatches the client's background and goals.

Examples:
  [Pass] Investor client asks about a property. Response includes
         rental income potential and cap rate.
  [Fail] Investor client asks about a property. Response focuses
         entirely on school districts and family amenities.
```

This rubric seems reasonable—it defines the criterion, explains the intent, and provides examples. But it leaves important questions unanswered. What if a response includes *both* investment metrics and family-oriented language? What counts as “tailored"--is mentioning one relevant detail enough, or does the entire response need to match? How should annotators handle responses that are neutral and generic rather than actively mismatched?

After independent labeling, your disagreement summary might look like this:

```
Trace #23: Investor client asked about a duplex. Assistant responded:
           "This duplex is in a growing neighborhood with a strong
           rental market. The 3-bed/2-bath layout is popular with
           families, and the backyard is a nice bonus. Current tenant
           pays $2,400/month."

  - Annotator A: Pass (mentions rental income and market strength)
  - Annotator B: Fail (the "popular with families" and "backyard"
                 language is inappropriate for an investor)
  - Annotator C: Pass (the family language describes tenant appeal,
                 which is relevant to rental investment)
```

All three annotators applied the rubric in good faith, but they reached different conclusions. Annotator A focused on whether investment-relevant information was present. Annotator B flagged the family-oriented language as a mismatch. Annotator C interpreted that same language as relevant investment context (explaining why the property attracts tenants). The rubric did not specify how to weigh these competing considerations, so each annotator made their own judgment call.

*Appoint a neutral moderator.* Someone needs to keep the discussion focused on improving the rubric rather than winning arguments about specific labels. Ideally, the moderator is not one of the annotators—perhaps a project lead or someone from an adjacent team who understands the product but did not participate in labeling.

## Running the Session

The goal of an alignment session is not to force agreement on past labels. The goal is to understand why annotators interpreted the rubric differently and to fix the rubric so future annotations will be consistent.

For each disagreement, work through these questions:

What did each annotator think?

Have each person explain their reasoning without interruption. In the Trace #23 example, Annotator C might say: “I labeled it Pass because the family-oriented language was explaining tenant appeal—that’s useful information for an investor deciding whether the property will stay rented.” Annotator B might counter: “But the rubric says the response should be tailored to the client. *Popular with families* and *nice backyard* sound like you’re selling to a homebuyer, not an investor.” Both interpretations are defensible, which reveals that the rubric needs to address this ambiguity.

Where does the rubric fail?

Identify the specific word, phrase, or gap in the rubric that allowed different interpretations. In Trace #23, the rubric said the response should be “tailored to the client’s background and goals” but did not specify whether language that serves a secondary purpose (describing tenant demographics) qualifies as tailored or mismatched. The rubric also lacked guidance on how to handle mixed responses that contain both investor-relevant and family-oriented content.

What would make future annotators agree?

Focus on forward-looking changes, not retrospective blame. For Trace #23, the team might decide to add: “Family-oriented language is acceptable if it explicitly connects to investment value (e.g., *popular with families, which keeps vacancy rates low*). Family-oriented language without investment framing should be marked Fail.”

Timebox each disagreement to prevent the session from dragging on. Five minutes per case is usually enough—if you cannot resolve it quickly, flag it for follow-up and move on.

## Techniques for Improving the Rubric

Based on the disagreements you uncover, you can improve the rubric in several ways:

Clarify wording

Vague or overloaded terms cause most disagreements. In the real estate assistant example, “tailored to the client’s background and goals” turned out to be ambiguous. Annotators disagreed about whether describing tenant demographics qualifies as investor-tailored content. The revised rubric should make the boundaries explicit:

```
Pass: The response focuses on information relevant to the client's goals:
  - Investors: Financial metrics (cap rate, rental income, appreciation),
    market conditions, tenant stability, and property condition.
    Descriptions of tenant demographics are acceptable only if explicitly
    connected to investment value.
  - First-time buyers: Affordability, financing options, neighborhood
    livability, and process guidance.

Fail: The response includes content mismatched to the client's goals
      without connecting it to their actual needs.
```

Add examples

Concrete Pass/Fail examples are often clearer than abstract definitions. When a disagreement reveals an edge case, add it to the rubric as an illustrative example. After the Trace #23 discussion, you might add:

```
Examples:
  [Pass] Client is an investor. Response: "This duplex offers a 6.2% cap
         rate with stable rental history. The 3-bed layout is popular
         with long-term family tenants, which keeps turnover low."
  [Fail] Client is an investor. Response: "This duplex is in a great
         neighborhood. The 3-bed layout is popular with families, and
         the backyard is a nice bonus." (No connection to investment value)
  [Pass] Client is a first-time buyer. Response: "This home qualifies for
         FHA loans, which means you could put down as little as 3.5%."
  [Fail] Client is a first-time buyer. Response: "The cap rate is
         suboptimal but the IRR looks promising if you refi in year three."
```

Add decision rules

For tricky situations, provide explicit rules. For example: “If the response mentions family-oriented features (backyard, school district, kid-friendly) to an investor client, label as Pass only if the response explicitly connects those features to rental demand, tenant stability, or property value. Otherwise, label as Fail.”

Split the criterion

Sometimes disagreements reveal that a single rubric is trying to capture multiple distinct concepts. If annotators consistently agree on whether financial metrics are present but disagree on whether lifestyle language is acceptable, consider splitting “client persona match” into two separate criteria: “includes relevant information” and “avoids mismatched framing.” Measuring agreement separately for each criterion will tell you which one needs more work.

Acknowledge irreducible ambiguity

Some cases are genuinely borderline. A response that mentions “good schools nearby” to an investor—without elaboration—might be interpreted as either a rental-demand signal or mismatched homebuyer language. If reasonable people could legitimately disagree, document the ambiguity and establish a tiebreaker rule (for example, “if the connection to investment value is implied but not explicit, label as Pass”).

## What to Avoid

Do not rely on majority voting to fix the rubric. Voting can resolve individual label disagreements, but voting does not improve the rubric. If three annotators vote 2-1 on a trace, you have resolved that trace but learned nothing about why they disagreed. Use voting only as a last resort for finalizing labels after the rubric is stable.

Do not let discussions become adversarial. The goal is to improve the rubric, not to prove that one annotator was “right.” Frame disagreements as information about where the rubric needs work.

Do not skip documentation. Write down every rubric change and the reasoning behind it. Future annotators (and future you) will need this context.

## Escalation

Sometimes annotators cannot reach agreement even after discussion. When this happens, escalate to a project lead or senior domain expert who can make a binding decision. Document the ruling and add it to the rubric. Persistent disagreement often indicates that the criterion itself is poorly defined or that the annotators have fundamentally different values—both of which are important to surface.

## Save Everything

Even after you have trained or prompted an automated evaluator, keep your gold-standard dataset and documentation. You may need them later for:

- Validating automated evaluators. When you build an LLM-as-Judge or other automated evaluator (covered in the next chapter), you need ground truth labels to measure whether the evaluator is accurate. The gold-standard dataset provides that ground truth.
- Fine-tuning. If you decide to train a specialized LLM, the labeled data becomes training data.
- Auditing. If stakeholders question your evaluation methodology, you can point to the rigorous process that produced your rubrics and labels.
- Iteration. As your system evolves, you may need to update your evaluation criteria. The documentation of past decisions helps you understand why the rubric looks the way it does.

## Common Pitfalls

Collaborative evaluation is powerful, but it requires careful management. Here are the most common ways teams go wrong.

## Skipping Independent Annotation

If annotators discuss traces or the rubric extensively before labeling independently, their judgments become correlated. They converge on a shared interpretation through discussion rather than by applying the rubric as written. This makes subsequent IAA measurements meaningless—you are measuring groupthink, not rubric clarity.

The whole point of independent annotation is to detect ambiguity. If annotators coordinate beforehand, you lose this signal. They might achieve high agreement, but a new annotator given only the rubric might interpret it completely differently.

Note that if you have decided to use the benevolent dictator model (a single annotator), this pitfall does not apply—there is only one annotator, so independence is not an issue. But if you have multiple annotators, their initial labels must be independent.

## Starting with a Vague Rubric

If your initial rubric is overly vague or incomplete, the first annotation round will produce low agreement and frustrate your annotators. Some vagueness is unavoidable—you cannot anticipate every edge case—but you should invest effort in the initial draft. Include clear definitions and at least a few concrete examples before asking people to start labeling. For example, whe real estate assistant example earlier in this chapter illustrates how a rubric that says only “appropriate for the client type” invites three different interpretations, while a rubric that breaks “appropriate” into vocabulary, tone, and detail level produces much more consistent labels.

## Using Only Percent Agreement

Percent agreement is intuitive but misleading when label distributions are skewed. Teams often celebrate 90% agreement without realizing that chance alone would produce 80% agreement given their label distribution. Always compute Cohen’s Kappa (or an appropriate alternative) to understand whether agreement exceeds chance.

## Focusing on Past Labels Instead of Future Consistency

Alignment sessions can easily devolve into arguments about whether a specific trace “should have been” Pass or Fail. This is unproductive. The goal is to improve the rubric so that future annotators will agree, not to relitigate past decisions. Keep discussions focused on: “What change to the rubric would have made us agree on this case?”

## Failing to Close the Loop

Teams sometimes invest heavily in collaborative annotation, produce beautiful rubrics and carefully labeled datasets, and then… do nothing with them. The rubric sits in a document that no one reads. The labels are never used to validate automated evaluators.

The value of collaborative annotation comes from using its outputs. Translate the rubric into LLM-as-Judge prompts. Use the gold-standard labels to measure judge accuracy. Update your automated evaluators based on what you learn. Without this follow-through, the effort is wasted.

## Putting It Together

Collaborative evaluation addresses a fundamental challenge: human judgment is often inconsistent, and many evaluation criteria are inherently subjective. By involving multiple annotators, measuring their agreement, and refining rubrics through structured discussion, you can develop evaluation criteria that are clear, consistent, and reliable.

The key outputs of this process are:

- A *refined rubric* that captures shared understanding of what good and bad outputs look like
- A *gold-standard labeled dataset* with consensus labels that serve as trustworthy ground truth
- *Documentation* of decisions and edge cases that helps maintain consistency over time

These outputs directly support the rest of the evaluation lifecycle. Clearer rubrics make LLM-as-Judge evaluators, as we will discuss in the next chapter, more accurate. Reliable gold-standard labels enable meaningful validation of those evaluators. Together, they provide the foundation for measuring system performance at scale and tracking improvement over time.

In summary, when a single expert’s judgment is sufficient—because the domain is narrow, the criteria are clear, and the expert has time—use the benevolent dictator model. It is simpler and faster. But when subjectivity, complexity, or scale demand multiple perspectives, the collaborative workflow in this chapter will help you build evaluation infrastructure that you can trust.
