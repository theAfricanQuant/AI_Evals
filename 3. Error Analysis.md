The process of developing robust evaluations for LLM applications is inherently iterative. It involves creating test cases, assessing performance, and refining the system based on those observations. High-level guides, such as Anthropic’s documentation on creating empirical evaluations for Claude Anthropic 2024, often depict the evaluation process as a cycle of developing test cases, engineering prompts, testing, and refining ([Figure 3-1](#fig-anthropic-diagram)).[^1] This chapter covers the *Analyze* step of our Analyze-Measure-Improve lifecycle ([Figure 1-2](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/evals-for-ai/9798341660717/ch01.html#fig-eval-lifecycle)). We walk through how to bootstrap an initial dataset, read traces, and build a structured understanding of where our application fails.

To make the discussion concrete, we use a running example: a real estate CRM assistant. This assistant is built to help real estate agents handle the everyday, often messy tasks that come up when they work with clients. A real estate professional might ask for help searching listings for a buyer, following up with warm leads, or preparing a quick market analysis. They often express these needs in conversational, ambiguous language—describing the kind of home a buyer wants, asking for “something affordable near downtown,” or checking whether any open houses are available soon. The assistant must interpret these requests, translate them into the right database queries or tool calls, and sometimes chain together multiple steps without losing track of constraints.

The pipeline is “agentic;” that is, the LLM decides what action to take next based on the current state of the interaction and whatever information it has already gathered. The model interprets the user’s request and produces a structured action such as querying listings, drafting an email, or checking the showings calendar. Each action triggers a downstream tool, and the tool’s output is passed back to the LLM. The model may then issue another action, refine an earlier one, or move on to the next step. This continues until the system has completed all necessary operations for the task.

## Establishing Terminology

Before moving forward, here are a few terms that recur throughout this chapter. When a user interacts with the system, they provide a *query*, or the raw text of their request. Everything that happens afterward, including the first model response all the way to the final output, constitutes a *trace*. A *trace* is the complete, ordered record of all steps the system took in response to the query. This includes every LLM message, every tool call, every value returned from a tool, and the final response shown to the user.

When we evaluate LLM outputs, we evaluate traces rather than individual steps. This is because the behavior of an LLM pipeline only makes sense when viewed end-to-end. An individual message might appear reasonable in isolation, yet still lead to an incorrect final output. Reading the full trace lets us identify where problems actually originate rather than mislabeling later symptoms as independent failures.

Throughout this chapter, “query” refers to the user’s original input, and “trace” refers to the entire sequence of actions the system produced in response.

## Creating a Starting Dataset of Traces

The first step is to assemble a dataset of traces. The purpose of this dataset is to give us concrete material to analyze, or real examples of how the system behaves in response to user requests. Without traces, we have nothing to observe, and error analysis cannot begin. We want our starting dataset to contain roughly 100 traces. This gives enough coverage to surface a range of failure modes and push toward *theoretical saturation*, or the point at which analyzing additional traces is unlikely to reveal new categories of errors, because existing patterns are well-developed and new data largely confirms them.

Theoretical saturation is a concept borrowed from qualitative research. We frame error analysis as building a taxonomy of failure modes for our system: a structured set of categories that describe the different ways our system can go wrong. When building such a taxonomy, we keep sampling and analyzing until new traces stop teaching us anything new. If we stop too early, we risk missing important failure modes that would have surfaced with more data. If we continue long past saturation, we waste effort analyzing traces that only confirm what we already know. Some systems saturate earlier; more complex agentic pipelines may require more exploration. The key is to keep analyzing until new failure modes stop appearing.

There are two ways to build the starting dataset of traces:

From production logs (preferred when available)

If you already have production traces, we can sample them directly. You want to ensure your sample is not just repeating the same use case, so you should try to include traces that cover different features or types of user input. For small datasets, random sampling works fine. For larger datasets, you can use clustering (for example, on query text embeddings or keyword matches) to select queries from different groups.

From synthetic queries

If you do not yet have traces available, you should first create a dataset of synthetic queries, then run your application on those queries to produce traces.

When generating synthetic queries, a common mistake is to simply prompt an LLM to “generate user queries for my application.” This approach produces queries that are generic, repetitive, and fail to capture real usage patterns. For example, prompting an LLM with “Generate 50 queries for a real estate assistant” might yield variations like:

- *Find me a house in San Francisco.*
- *Show me homes in San Francisco.*
- *I’m looking for a property in San Francisco.*
- *Can you find houses in the San Francisco area?*

These queries all test the same basic capability. They do not vary along dimensions that matter for your application—dimensions that, if left unexplored, could hide entire categories of failure modes. For example, if all your synthetic queries are well-specified property searches, you will never discover that your assistant fails when users make ambiguous requests. Running your pipeline on repetitive queries will produce traces that all look similar, and you will miss failures that only surface under different conditions.

A better approach to generating synthetic queries is to add a layer of indirection: instead of asking an LLM to generate queries directly, first define the dimensions along which queries can vary, then generate structured combinations of those dimensions, and finally convert each combination into a natural-language query. This separation forces diversity in a controlled way and prevents the LLM from collapsing into repetitive outputs.

## Defining Dimensions

A dimension is simply a category of variation—one axis along which requests differ from each other. For the real estate assistant, useful dimensions might include:

Feature

what task the user wants to perform (e.g., property search, scheduling, email drafting)

Client Persona

the type of client being served (e.g., first-time buyer, investor, luxury buyer)

Scenario Type

how clearly the user expresses their intent (e.g., well-specified, ambiguous, out-of-scope)

Identifying good dimensions often requires some effort to understand the product better. Talk to product managers, review user feedback, or spend time using the application yourself. That said, you do not need to enumerate every possible dimension before getting started. Once you have three sufficiently diverse dimensions, begin generating data. You can always add more dimensions later as your understanding deepens.

When choosing dimensions, focus on ones that describe where your application is likely to fail. If you do not yet know where your product might fail, build intuition by using it yourself, or recruit a few people to use it and let their failures motivate the dimensions.

## Generating Tuples and Queries

Once you have defined your dimensions, you can use an LLM to generate synthetic queries, but with a layer of indirection that ensures diversity. First, create structured combinations of your dimensions. A tuple is a specific combination of values, one from each dimension, that defines a particular use case. For example:

- Feature: property search
- Client Persona: investor
- Scenario Type: ambiguous

This tuple describes a case where an investor is searching for properties, but the request is underspecified or vague. From this tuple, you might generate a query like: “Look up showings for good properties in San Mateo County.” A different tuple with a first-time buyer and a specific scenario might yield: “Find 3-bedroom homes under $600k near downtown that allow pets.”

Writing 20 tuples by hand helps build understanding of the problem space. If you are unsure what tuples to write, consult domain experts or review real user queries for inspiration. Once you have a small set of example tuples and corresponding queries, use an LLM to scale up to 100 or more.

The key idea is to separate tuple generation from query generation. First, prompt the LLM to generate structured tuples. Then, for each tuple, prompt the LLM to generate a natural-language query. This two-step process works better than asking an LLM to generate queries directly, because the tuples constrain each query to cover a specific combination of dimensions.

```
Generate 10 random combinations of (feature, client persona, scenario) for a real estate CRM assistant.

The dimensions are:

Feature: what task the agent wants to perform. Possible values: property
search, market analysis, scheduling, email drafting.

Client persona: the type of client the agent is working with. Possible
values: first-time homebuyer, investor, luxury buyer.

Scenario: how well-formed or challenging the query is. Possible values:
- exact match (clearly specified and feasible),
- ambiguous request (unclear or underspecified),
- shouldn't be handled (invalid or out-of-scope).

Output each tuple in the format:
(feature, client persona, scenario)

Avoid duplicates. Vary values across dimensions.
```

For each tuple, prompt the LLM to generate a corresponding natural-language query:

```
We are generating synthetic user queries for a real estate CRM assistant.
The assistant helps agents manage client requests by searching listings,
analyzing markets, drafting emails, and reading calendars.

Given:

Feature: Scheduling
Client Persona: First-time homebuyer
Scenario: Ambiguous request

Write a realistic query that an agent might enter into the system to
fulfill this client's request. The query should reflect the client's needs
and the ambiguity of the scenario.

Example:
"Find showings for affordable homes with short notice availability."

Now generate a new query.
```

Continue this process of sampling, generating, and filtering until you have around 100 high-quality, diverse examples. If the phrasing is awkward or the content misses the mark, you can throw the query out and regenerate it. It is worth being aggressive about quality control at this stage, because future steps of error analysis depend on how representative and realistic these traces are.

Once you have curated your queries, run each one through your application end-to-end and export the full trace. The export should include all LLM steps, tool calls, inputs, and outputs. If you are using an observability platform like Braintrust, LangSmith, or Weave, you can export traces directly from their interfaces. If you have a simpler setup, you can log each step to a JSON file or a database as the agent executes. For the purposes of annotation, many teams find it easiest to export traces to a spreadsheet, with one row per trace and columns for the query, each intermediate step, and the final output. The format matters less than having a complete record that you can read and annotate.

With these traces in hand, you are ready to start reading them and identifying failure modes.

## Open Coding: Reading and Labeling Traces

Now, the next step is to read through your traces and annotate them with observations about what went wrong. The goal is to surface failure patterns without imposing a predefined structure. You let the categories emerge from what you actually see in the data.

Our labeling process is adapted from *grounded theory* (\[Link to Come\], Glaser and Strauss 2017, \[Link to Come\], Strauss and Corbin 1990), a methodology from qualitative research that builds taxonomies directly from data. In grounded theory, *coding* refers to assigning short descriptive labels to parts of the data that seem important. For each trace, you read carefully and write brief notes about what you observe. You might note where outputs are incorrect, where actions are surprising, or where the behavior feels wrong. Each note is a potential signal of a failure mode. These annotations can be short, a phrase or a sentence or two, and do not need to follow any particular format. The goal is to capture your observations quickly. In later steps of error analysis, we will categorize and structure these observations more rigorously.

When open coding, it is important to put yourself in the perspective of the user. Is the user actually having a good experience? Does the response address their request? At this stage, focus on noticing and recording what feels wrong rather than diagnosing causes or thinking about fixes.

A common question we get is: should you note down all the failures in a trace, or just the first one? We recommend starting with the first failure you observe, which is usually the most upstream point where something went wrong. Traces can be complex, and a single upstream error often causes a cascade of downstream issues. Focusing on the first failure is more efficient and prevents you from cataloging every symptom when many are just consequences of a single root cause. In many cases, fixing the first error resolves the entire chain.

You can record each trace and its corresponding annotation in a simple table or spreadsheet, with one column for the trace identifier, another for the trace itself or a summary, and a third for your freeform notes describing the first failure.

In practice, open codes are short, lowercase notes jotted down as you read. They do not need to be polished or grammatical, but they should include enough context that a teammate reading them later can understand what went wrong without re-reading the whole trace. Here are some realistic examples of the kinds of notes you might write while reading traces from the real estate assistant.

| Trace situation | Open code |
| --- | --- |
| User asked for “3-bedroom homes under $600k near downtown that allow pets.” Assistant generated SQL with filters on price and bedroom count, returned 2 listings, and drafted an email to the client. | sql query dropped the pet-friendly filter even though the user explicitly asked for it; downstream listings and email all silently miss the constraint |
| User asked to “set up showings for these two homes this weekend.” Calendar API returned the agent as unavailable Saturday afternoon. Assistant proposed showings for both Saturday 2pm and Sunday 11am. | assistant proposed a saturday slot that the calendar already showed as blocked; treated the calendar result as advisory instead of a hard constraint |
| User asked to “send a property list to my investor client in San Mateo.” Assistant searched for high-ROI properties, but drafted a casual, emoji-heavy email pitching them as great starter homes. | tone and framing don’t match an investor persona; email reads like it was written for a first-time buyer even though the search itself was investor-appropriate |
| Listing record in the database says “2 bed, 1 bath.” Assistant’s draft email describes the home as a “spacious 3-bedroom with a renovated second bath.” | hallucinated bedroom count and bathroom upgrade; details look plausible but contradict the listing data the assistant just retrieved |
| User said “send the shortlist to my client.” Assistant sent the email to a contact from a different deal that came up earlier in the conversation history. | wrong recipient; assistant grabbed the most recently mentioned client name instead of the one tied to the active shortlist |
| User asked about “Springfield” listings. The agent’s location context is Massachusetts. Assistant returned results from Springfield, IL without asking which one. | ambiguous city name resolved silently to the wrong state; no clarifying question even though multiple springfields exist |
| User asked, “what’s a good offer price for 142 Oak St?” Assistant answered “$585k” with no comps lookup or market analysis tool call in the trace. | assistant produced a price recommendation without ever pulling comps; answer is confidently stated but not grounded in any retrieved data |
| During a multi-step request, the listings search tool returned an error payload. Assistant ignored the error and proceeded to draft an email summarizing “the homes I found.” | tool error ignored without being surfaced or retried; assistant kept going and fabricated results instead |
| User asked for “a quick summary of this week’s leads.” Reply is six paragraphs with section headers. | response is much longer than “quick summary” implied; ignored the brevity hint in the user’s wording |
| User asked the assistant to “draft an email” to a client. Assistant drafted the email and immediately called send\_email without showing the draft for review. | assistant took an irreversible action (sending the email) when the user had only asked it to draft; skipped the implicit confirmation step |

Notice that these notes are not categories yet. They are raw observations, written in whatever phrasing came to mind while reading. Some will turn out to be instances of the same underlying failure mode, and some will stand alone. That sorting happens later, in axial coding.

During open coding, do not try to group or formalize errors. Focus on collecting a rich set of raw observations. You can also assign each trace a holistic binary judgment, *acceptable* or *unacceptable*, as a fourth column. Making a clear yes-or-no decision forces sharper thinking than vague scores, and even when the judgment feels borderline, picking a side helps clarify what you consider a failure.

Sometimes it can be difficult to articulate precisely what feels wrong about a trace. If you find yourself stuck, one strategy is to temporarily adopt a more “top-down” approach: instead of waiting for failure modes to emerge from your error analysis, consider common LLM failure categories and actively look for their manifestations. For example, knowing that hallucination is a common issue, you might specifically check each trace for instances where the assistant invents facts or misrepresents information. You can also draw on failure modes you have heard about that are specific to your application. If you are building a text-to-SQL system, you might look for malformed queries or incorrect joins. If you are building a customer service bot, you might look for responses that contradict company policy. The goal is not to force traces into predefined categories, but to use known failure types as “lenses” that help you identify problems that might otherwise be hard to pin down.

How long should open coding continue? A useful heuristic is to proceed until you have labeled at least 20 problematic traces and new failure modes have stopped appearing. At that point, you likely have enough raw material to move on to structuring your observations.

## Axial Coding: Structuring Failure Modes

Open coding produces a valuable but unstructured collection of observations. Each trace has its own raw notes about missing constraints, invalid actions, inappropriate tones, or hallucinated facts. To make sense of these observations and quantify how often different problems occur, you need to organize them into a structured set of failure modes.

This is where *axial coding* comes in, another concept from grounded theory. Axial coding is the process of identifying relationships among the notes you generated during open coding and grouping them into higher-order categories. It moves you from isolated observations to a coherent set of distinct, recurring failure types.

To axial code, read through your open-coded traces and cluster similar annotations together. Some patterns are obvious. For example, traces where the assistant ignores a buyer’s budget or omits a requirement like number of bedrooms cluster naturally into a broader failure mode: *violation of user constraints*. Other patterns show deeper distinctions only after reading several traces. For example, hallucinations of property features (claiming a home has solar panels when it does not) and hallucinations of client activity (scheduling a tour that the user never requested) might initially seem like the same problem. But these failures have different causes and require different fixes. Hallucinated property features likely stem from the model inventing details not present in the listing data, while hallucinated client activity suggests the model is misinterpreting or fabricating user intent. You would split them into *hallucinated listing metadata* and *hallucinated user actions* because diagnosing and addressing them requires different strategies. That said, this is just a first pass. It is fine if you end up splitting or merging categories later as your understanding of the failure modes deepens.

While early clustering is often done manually, you can also use a language model to help organize your notes. Note that we do not recommend using an LLM for open coding itself, because that step requires your judgment and taste about what constitutes a failure. But for axial coding, an LLM can be useful: its job is not to apply your taste, but to analyze the observations you have already made and propose a way to organize them. After open coding traces, you can paste the raw open codes into an LLM, along with the traces themsleves, and ask the LLM to propose preliminary groupings:

```
Below is a list of open-ended annotations describing failures in an
LLM-driven real estate CRM assistant. Please group them into a small set
of coherent failure categories, where each category captures similar types of mistakes. Each group should have a short descriptive title and a brief
one-line definition. Do not invent new failure types; only cluster based
on what is present in the notes.
```

LLM-generated groupings can help organize initial ideas, but they should not be accepted blindly. The LLM does not know how your system works or how you would go about fixing different types of failures, so its proposed categories may merge issues that should be kept separate or split issues that belong together. You will almost always need to review the taxonomy and make edits yourself: renaming categories, merging ones that are too similar, or splitting ones that lump together failures with different root causes.

At the end of axial coding, you have a list of distinct failure modes, along with representative examples illustrating each one. These examples serve as reference points as you move into the next stage of error analysis: systematically labeling and quantifying failures across all traces.

## Labeling Traces with Structured Failure Modes

At this point, you have two artifacts:

1. A collection of traces, each with its initial freeform annotation from open coding.
2. A defined list of structured failure modes from axial coding.

The next step is to go back through your traces and label each one with the failure modes that apply. This bridges the gap from qualitative observation to quantitative data. For each trace, compare its open codes against your list of failure modes and determine which ones apply. If you derived the failure modes manually during axial coding, this is straightforward: you apply the mapping you developed. If an LLM assisted in generating the categories, you need to ensure those categories are consistently applied back to each trace. You can do this manually, or you can prompt an LLM with each trace’s annotation and your list of failure modes and ask it to assign the appropriate labels. When using an LLM for this step, make sure you review its assignments to catch mistakes and refine definitions where needed.

In your spreadsheet, add a new column for each failure mode. For each trace, mark a 1 if that failure is present and a 0 if it is absent. For example, if a first-pass annotation was “SQL query missed the budget constraint and the email tone was too aggressive,” and your failure modes include “Missing Constraint” and “Inappropriate Tone,” this trace would receive a 1 in both columns and 0s in the others. You can think of this as preparing your data for a pivot table: once the columns are populated, you can easily aggregate and slice by failure mode.

As you work through this process, you will likely discover edge cases. Some traces may not cleanly match any defined failure mode. Others may reveal nuances that suggest a category needs to be refined or split. It is fine to adjust your failure mode definitions as you go.

Once labeling is complete, you can count the occurrences of each failure mode and divide by the total number of traces to get a prevalence rate. You can do this with a simple formula in a spreadsheet, or with tools like Pandas or pivot tables if you want to slice the data by different dimensions. This tells you which failures are most common, which is useful for deciding where to focus improvement efforts.

## Iteration and Refinement

Error analysis is not a one-time process. It is common to go through two or three rounds of reviewing traces and refining your failure mode taxonomy. Early rounds often uncover failure modes that were initially missed, or reveal that your definitions need adjustment. As you analyze more traces, whether by labeling ones you skipped earlier or by sampling new queries, your understanding of the data deepens and the taxonomy becomes more precise.

Refinement takes different forms. Sometimes you merge categories that turn out to be too similar to distinguish reliably. Other times you split a category that was lumping together failures with different root causes. And sometimes you simply clarify a definition to make it easier to apply consistently.

For example, a second round of analysis on the real estate CRM assistant might expose a new error that was not apparent in the first batch: the assistant occasionally misinterprets location names by defaulting to a local city without asking for clarification. A user asking about “Springfield” listings might receive results from the wrong state entirely. This would lead you to add *location ambiguity errors* as a distinct failure mode.

Iteration is not endless. In practice, two serious rounds of open coding and re-annotation are often sufficient to approach theoretical saturation. Beyond that point, additional error analysis on the same dataset typically yields diminishing returns. That said, error analysis is something we always return to periodically. After fixing errors, shipping to more users, or observing new patterns of user behavior, it is worth cycling back through the process with fresh data. The value of error analysis comes from engaging with what your system is actually doing now, not from repeatedly analyzing a static dataset.

## Common Pitfalls

There is no single correct way to do error analysis. You may find that you want to spend more time on some stages than others—it depends on your team’s working style, and how difficult your application is to evaluate. However, there are many bad ways to do error analysis. We list some common pitfalls in this section.

The most common mistake in early error analysis is failing to test on representative data. If your initial query set does not reflect the diversity and difficulty of real user behavior, the traces you produce will be uninformative. Either no serious failures occur, or the failures that do appear are not the ones that would arise in production.

A second common mistake is skipping open coding altogether. Instead of reading real traces and observing how the system actually fails, teams often default to generic categories pulled from LLM research, such as “hallucination,” “instruction following,” and “verbosity.” These labels may sound reasonable, but without grounding in real examples, they often miss application-specific issues. In the real estate CRM assistant, for instance, errors like proposing unavailable showings or misidentifying client personas are far more damaging than generic verbosity. Many evaluation vendors promote off-the-shelf metrics, which can lead engineers toward metrics that sound useful but do not capture what actually matters for their application.

Another frequent pitfall is the inappropriate use of Likert scales during early annotation. A Likert scale asks annotators to rate qualities like relevance or helpfulness on a numeric scale, typically 1 to 5. While widely used, Likert scales introduce substantial noise when applied without a detailed rubric. When rubrics are underspecified, rating scales lead to lower agreement between annotators and higher subjective variance. In contrast, forcing binary decisions about specific failure modes—whether a problem occurred or not—produces more reproducible annotations.

Finally, treating initial annotations and failure modes as fixed is a mistake. It is normal for annotation schemas to evolve after reviewing more data. New examples may reveal edge cases that require refining definitions. Freezing the schema too early locks your evaluation infrastructure around an incomplete understanding of system behavior. Relatedly, excluding domain experts from the labeling process is a critical misstep, especially for applications that require deep subject matter knowledge. Outsourcing annotation to people without domain expertise often leads to superficial or incorrect labels. If domain experts find your annotation tools cumbersome, it is worth investing in simpler interfaces that make their participation easier.

## Putting It Together

The Analyze phase is the foundation of effective LLM evaluation. It provides the qualitative understanding necessary before any meaningful measurement or improvement can occur.

We began by emphasizing the importance of assembling a diverse initial dataset of traces, using real user queries when available or a structured method for generating representative synthetic queries when logs are sparse. We then walked through the process of reading and labeling traces using techniques adapted from grounded theory: open coding to surface raw observations, and axial coding to cluster those observations into a coherent taxonomy of failure modes. Finally, we described how to apply structured labels to traces and quantify the prevalence of each failure mode.

You should come away from this chapter with a clear vocabulary of failure: a consistent set of failure modes that let you describe precisely how and why your pipeline isn’t working. That foundation is essential before you can measure anything at scale.
