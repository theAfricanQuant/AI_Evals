Issue 06 Evaluating multi-turn conversations Trace · Test · Teach
Lead story · Paired control and replay

Three paired tests. Three matching labels.

In these small comparisons, changing when a requirement arrived did not change the recorded verdict within any pair.

Chapter 6 asks whether an assistant keeps track of requirements delivered across several turns. A control gives the requirements together; a replay delivers them over a conversation. Three same-model Gemini 3.8 pairs received matching labels within each pair. The course lesson frames this as a conversation-level evaluation.

The predeclared rule judged each final answer against a fixed text-only rubric: all explicit user constraints had to be met for Pass; a visible violation meant Fail; unclear text could be marked Review. The first two pairs were Pass/Pass; the third was Fail/Fail. The paired replay records and rubric document the setups and labels.

The idea in plain language

Change the timing. Hold the comparison steady.

Within each pair, the model and scoring rule stayed the same. The control put the user requirements in one request; the replay spread them across turns. Matching labels are an observation about these runs, not proof that turn structure never matters.

Briefs · Method and limits

What the pairs can tell us

Judge the whole conversation

The session verdict asks whether the user’s goal was met by the end. Pipeline-stage labels help diagnose where a failure began; they are not interchangeable with the final conversation verdict. The trace note records that distinction.

The rubric matters

A text-only rule made the final labels observable. It did not assess broader health or safety claims. The paired rubric defines the limits.

A matched pair is not a general rule

One response per condition leaves run-to-run variation unmeasured. The results show matching labels in these three comparisons only. The lab record describes the evidence and limits.
